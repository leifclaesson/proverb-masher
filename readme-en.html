Mashed-up Proverbs — English Edition

Provenance. English remaster of COOKIE.BAS by Leif Claesson (Liket of Goto10), ~1996, written in PowerBASIC for DOS and recovered decades later from a dig through old disks. Its data file ORDSPRÅK.TXT is served on this site byte-for-byte pristine, still CP437. The Swedish original's own page is in Swedish; the engine it describes is summarised below. A Thai edition and a Russian edition live here too (TH, RU).

Same engine, same joke: real proverbs are split by a pipe into a HEAD and a TAIL, sorted into groups of interchangeable halves, and the program glues one proverb's HEAD to a different proverb's TAIL. It is structurally incapable of printing an intact line — it can only ever be wrong.

A clear conscience is the finest headache. · New brooms run deep. · Don't count your chickens in public.

The engine, unchanged since 1996

A data file is one head|tail proverb per line, # ; ' for a comment, * on its own line to start the next group, and ** as a hard end-of-file (the author's three spares stay parked behind it, unreachable since 1996). Two details lift it above a dumb word-masher:

1. Population-weighted group choice. It does not pick a group uniformly. It builds a flat table grupp[] with one slot per proverb, each holding that proverb's group number, then picks a random slot — so a fat group is chosen in proportion to how many proverbs it holds, and the decoy-stuffed Group 1 dominates the output. The weighting is what makes the joke land reliably.

2. fanheller: is the retry label that rejects two identical rolls, forcing head and tail to come from different lines. The name is a Swedish curse (fan heller ≈ "like hell"), and per remaster tradition every edition swears in its own language.

The English-only problem: number agreement

Swedish verbs don't conjugate for number (barn leka, tunnor skramlar, en svala gör — the same verb form regardless), so the 1996 original could dump every subject+predicate proverb into one big Group 1 and any swap stayed grammatical. English can't: "Expensive dogs catches the worm" leaks the seam, and the whole joke is wrong meaning, right grammar.

The first fix was to split Group 1 by subject number and keep singular and plural apart. It worked, and it cost 5880 jokes: "Empty vessels dread the fire" and "The early bird makes the most noise" were unreachable by construction. So the split is gone, and the tails carry their own agreement instead:

In the data fileMeans
+ at the start of a linethis subject is PLURAL
(s) in a tailthere when the head is singular, gone when it's plural
(x/y) in a tailx when the head is singular, y when it's plural

The early bird|catch(es) the worm
+Empty vessels|make(s) the most noise
A leopard|never change(s) (its/their) spots

The tail carries both forms, so gluing a head onto it is one search-and-replace — no verb table, no morphology, nothing anywhere that knows any English. That direction is also the only one on offer: deriving a base form from an inflected one needs a dictionary, because "rises" cuts back to rise while "catches" cuts back to catch — same ending, different seam.

Verbs that don't inflect need no marking and already fit both: past tense ("killed the cat"), modals ("will clutch at a straw"). And two tails end in a plural noun, left deliberately to clash — a mismatched verb reads as a bug, but a mismatched noun reads as the joke:

A watched pot will be boys. · Curiosity can't be choosers.

So it's one group again — 119 lines, 14042 reachable pairs against 8162 before — and the decoys still dominate, because the weighted pick counts proverbs and merging two groups doesn't change how many there are.

Same trap, subtler form: a TAIL that is a relative clause re-agrees with its new head. "Don't bite the hand that feeds you" mashed onto "Don't count your chickens" gives "…chickens that feeds you" — broken. So the don't-group's tails are all adverbial ("in one basket", "over spilled milk", "in public"), which fit any head. When adding proverbs, test the worst pairing in the group, not the average one.

A pun the translation lost

The original's decoy word dyr and English dear are the same Germanic word — Old English dēore meant both "beloved" and "costly" ("it cost him dear"). This edition chose "expensive" because dear-as-pricey reads archaic today, making English the one edition that dropped half the pun. The Russian edition got it back for free: «дорогой» still means both.

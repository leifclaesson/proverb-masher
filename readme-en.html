Mashed-up Proverbs — English Edition

Provenance. English remaster of COOKIE.BAS by Leif Claesson (Liket of Goto10), ~1996. The Swedish original and its full engine writeup live in the SV README — read that first; this page only covers what the English transposition changed. A Thai edition and a Russian edition live here too (TH, RU).

Same engine, same joke: real proverbs are split by a pipe into a HEAD and a TAIL, grouped into sections of interchangeable halves, and the program glues one proverb's HEAD to a different proverb's TAIL. It is structurally incapable of printing an intact line — it can only ever be wrong.

A clear conscience is the finest headache. · New brooms run deep. · Don't count your chickens in public.

The English-only problem: number agreement

Swedish verbs don't conjugate for number (barn leka, tunnor skramlar, en svala gör — the same verb form regardless), so the 1996 original could dump every subject+predicate proverb into one big Group 1 and any swap stayed grammatical. English can't: "Expensive dogs catches the worm" leaks the seam, and the whole joke is wrong meaning, right grammar.

So Group 1 is split by subject number:

  • Group 1: singular subjects ("A watched pot", "The early bird", …) — still the biggest group, so the weighted pick still fires the decoys most.
  • Group 2: plural subjects ("Empty vessels", "Barking dogs", …) — with its own decoys.

Same trap, subtler form: a TAIL that is a relative clause re-agrees with its new head. "Don't bite the hand that feeds you" mashed onto "Don't count your chickens" gives "…chickens that feeds you" — broken. So the don't-group's tails are all adverbial ("in one basket", "over spilled milk", "in public"), which fit any head. When adding proverbs, test the worst pairing in the group, not the average one.

A pun the translation lost

The original's decoy word dyr and English dear are the same Germanic word — Old English dēore meant both "beloved" and "costly" ("it cost him dear"). This edition chose "expensive" because dear-as-pricey reads archaic today, making English the one edition that dropped half the pun. The Russian edition got it back for free: «дорогой» still means both.
